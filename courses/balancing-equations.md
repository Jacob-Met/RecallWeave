# Balancing equations: keep the formulas, check every element

This short casebook is for a learner who can multiply whole numbers and compare ratios, but wants to understand what a balanced chemical equation actually certifies. Work on paper or in your own notes. The formulas and products are supplied: your task is to check or construct coefficients, not to predict a reaction.

These are symbolic exercises with specified neutral formulas. A successful atom count does not establish that a reaction occurs, goes to completion, follows a particular mechanism, or has a particular rate or yield. No experimental method, charged-equation balancing or half-reaction method is included.

## Reading the notation

- An **element symbol** begins with a capital letter and may include a lowercase letter. Here H is hydrogen, C carbon, N nitrogen, O oxygen, Ca calcium, Al aluminium, Cl chlorine and S sulfur. Thus Ca is one symbol, not C followed by another element.
- A digit inside a formula is a **subscript**, written on the ordinary line in code-style notation here. In `O2`, the 2 belongs to O. An absent subscript means 1.
- A subscript after parentheses multiplies every atom count inside that group. Count the group first, then multiply. Do not apply it only to the group's final element.
- A **coefficient** is a number before a whole formula. It multiplies every element count in that formula. A missing coefficient means 1. Spaces between a coefficient and a formula are only for readability.
- A **skeleton** fixes the listed substances and their sides of the arrow. The plus sign separates substances; the arrow separates the left and right sides. Balance by changing coefficients only. Do not change subscripts, add or delete substances, or move them across the arrow.
- Each final coefficient in this casebook must be a **strictly positive whole number**. Zero would delete a listed substance, so it is not an allowed final coefficient. Fractions may be used during reasoning, but clear them by multiplying **every** coefficient by the same suitable whole number.
- After finding integer coefficients, divide **all** of them by their greatest common divisor (gcd), the largest positive whole number dividing them all. A gcd of 1 means that particular ratio has no common factor left to remove. It does not by itself prove that the fixed skeleton has only one possible positive ratio.
- A balance needs equal totals for **each element separately**. For an element appearing in several substances on one side, add all its contributions. Equal total atom counts without checking element identities are not enough.
- A molecular formula counts atoms in a molecule. An ionic compound's formula instead describes a formula unit, a ratio of its constituent ions; do not call every formula a discrete molecule. For Case 1, `Ca(NO3)2` is the formula of ionic calcium nitrate, while `NH3` describes an ammonia molecule. Formula counting alone does not specify all bonding or spatial structure.

For a verification table, use one row per element and columns for its left total and right total. Show the coefficient multiplications and sums, not only “balanced.” When reporting a coefficient vector, use the exact species order named in the question.

## Case 1 — Count the group, then the whole formula

Consider these four expressions separately:

| Expression | What the leading coefficient refers to |
|---|---|
| `Ca(NO3)2` | One formula unit |
| `2 Ca(NO3)2` | Two formula units |
| `NH3` | One molecule |
| `3 NH3` | Three molecules |

1. Make a table giving the represented counts of Ca, N, O and H in each expression. Write 0 for an element that is absent.
2. Explain the different jobs of the final 2 in `Ca(NO3)2` and the leading 2 in `2 Ca(NO3)2`. Which parts of the expression does each multiply?
3. A learner calls `2 Ca(NO3)2` “two calcium nitrate molecules” and says its formula is a complete structural drawing. Correct both claims without changing the formula or its counts.

## Case 2 — A count can pass while the fixed-species task fails

The assigned skeleton is:

**`CO + O2 → CO2`**

Here `O2` is molecular oxygen; `O` on its own would designate an oxygen atom instead.

Two proposed answers are:

- **A:** `2 CO + O2 → 2 CO2`
- **B:** `CO + O → CO2`

1. Give complete C and O totals on both sides of A and of B.
2. Which proposed answer satisfies both atom conservation and the assigned fixed-species rule? Identify exactly what, if anything, was changed illegally.
3. Explain why “the atom totals match” is not a sufficient reason to accept any rewritten equation as an answer to this particular skeleton.

## Case 3 — Construct coefficients and verify the sum

Keep this skeleton and this species order:

**`C2H4 + O2 → CO2 + H2O`**

1. Find strictly positive integer coefficients with no common factor. Report them in the order `(C2H4, O2, CO2, H2O)`, and write the resulting equation.
2. Give an element-by-element verification for C, H and O. Show both product contributions to the O total.
3. Explain one valid route from the C and H counts to the required O2 coefficient. Does the resulting balance prove an actual product yield or reaction rate?

## Case 4 — Clear fractions without changing the ratio

The fixed skeleton and coefficient order are:

**`Al + Cl2 → AlCl3`**, in order `(Al, Cl2, AlCl3)`.

Two students have reached these coefficient vectors:

- **P:** `(1, 3/2, 1)`
- **Q:** `(6, 9, 6)`

1. Check the Al and Cl totals for both P and Q. Distinguish an atom-balanced intermediate from a permitted final answer under this casebook's rules.
2. Convert P to whole-number coefficients by scaling every entry together. Normalize Q by its common divisor. Do the resulting vectors agree? Give a full atom-count check for the normalized result.
3. A third student replaces only the `3/2` in P with `3`, leaving the other two entries at 1. Check that new vector and explain why it is not the same operation as clearing all denominators.

## Case 5 — Parentheses within a complete balance

Keep all four formulas and this species order:

**`Ca(OH)2 + HNO3 → Ca(NO3)2 + H2O`**

1. Find positive integer coefficients with no common factor, in order `(Ca(OH)2, HNO3, Ca(NO3)2, H2O)`.
2. Verify Ca, N, H and O separately. For H and O, show every contributing substance on each side rather than treating the parenthesized groups as indivisible atoms.
3. Explain why reducing or changing the subscripts inside `Ca(OH)2` or `Ca(NO3)2` would not be a legitimate way to normalize the coefficients.

## Case 6 — Is this one bad attempt, or an impossible fixed skeleton?

The deliberately restricted skeleton is:

**`SO2 → SO3`**

Only these two formulas on these sides are allowed. Let a be the positive coefficient of SO2 and b the positive coefficient of SO3.

1. Write the equality required by S conservation and the equality required by O conservation.
2. Can both equalities hold with strictly positive a and b? Give a contradiction if not; trying one unsuccessful pair of numbers is not enough to establish impossibility.
3. Explain why either the all-zero vector or introducing another substance would fail to answer the stated question. Does impossibility for this restricted skeleton establish that SO3 can never participate in any chemical reaction?

## Case 7 — Removing a common factor is not a uniqueness proof

For this final symbolic skeleton, keep both listed products:

**`H2 + O2 → H2O + H2O2`**

Use coefficient order `(H2, O2, H2O, H2O2)`. Two proposed vectors are supplied:

- **U:** `(5, 3, 4, 1)`
- **V:** `(5, 4, 2, 3)`

1. Check H and O totals separately for U and V, and find the gcd of each vector.
2. Decide whether the two vectors are scalar multiples: could multiplying every entry of one by the same positive number produce the other? Use their actual entries to justify your answer.
3. Assess this claim: “Once all coefficients are positive integers with gcd 1, the skeleton must have exactly one such answer.” State what normalization does establish and what these two proposed vectors show.
4. Do either these counts or the existence of more than one admissible ratio determine what a real mixture of hydrogen and oxygen would produce? Keep the algebraic conclusion separate from that experimental question. You do not need to find all positive ratios.

After completing the questions, compare your reasoning with the worked answers. The next learner step is the existing [Reaction amounts: ratios, limits and leftovers](stoichiometry-foundations.md): it starts from a supplied balanced equation and uses its coefficients to compare mole amounts. This casebook stops before those amount and limiting-reactant calculations.

## Worked answers

### Case 1 — The two multipliers have different scopes

1. The represented atom counts are:

   | Expression | Ca | N | O | H |
   |---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
   | `Ca(NO3)2` | 1 | 2 | 6 | 0 |
   | `2 Ca(NO3)2` | 2 | 4 | 12 | 0 |
   | `NH3` | 0 | 1 | 0 | 3 |
   | `3 NH3` | 0 | 3 | 0 | 9 |

   In one `NO3` group there is one N and three O. Two such groups contribute two N and six O; the Ca outside the parentheses still has its implicit subscript 1.

2. The final 2 in `Ca(NO3)2` multiplies the entire parenthesized `NO3` group, including both N and O. It does not multiply Ca. The leading 2 in `2 Ca(NO3)2` multiplies the whole formula's counts: Ca, N and O all double. Likewise, the leading 3 in `3 NH3` multiplies both N and H counts, not just H.

3. The appropriate description is **two formula units of calcium nitrate**, rather than two discrete calcium nitrate molecules. The formula gives the represented composition ratio. It is not a complete structural drawing and does not specify all bonding or spatial arrangement. This terminology correction leaves the counts Ca = 2, N = 4 and O = 12 unchanged.

### Case 2 — Conservation and the fixed skeleton are separate checks

1. Both proposals have equal counts for each element:

   | Proposal | Element | Left total | Right total |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | A: `2 CO + O2 → 2 CO2` | C | 2 × 1 = 2 | 2 × 1 = 2 |
   | A | O | (2 × 1) + (1 × 2) = 4 | 2 × 2 = 4 |
   | B: `CO + O → CO2` | C | 1 × 1 = 1 | 1 × 1 = 1 |
   | B | O | (1 × 1) + (1 × 1) = 2 | 1 × 2 = 2 |

2. **Only A answers the assigned skeleton.** It keeps CO, O2 and CO2 on their specified sides and changes only coefficients. B replaces the required O2 with O. That is a change from a molecular-oxygen formula to an atomic-oxygen symbol, not a change in the quantity of the original substance.

3. An equation can conserve elements while representing different listed substances. The task therefore has two checks: preserve the supplied formulas and sides, then verify atom conservation through coefficients. Passing the second check does not excuse failing the first.

### Case 3 — Build one ratio, then recount every element

1. A normalized coefficient vector in the requested order is **`(1, 3, 2, 2)`**:

   **`C2H4 + 3 O2 → 2 CO2 + 2 H2O`**

   Its gcd is 1, so this ratio has no common integer factor left to remove.

2. The full check is:

   | Element | Left total | Right total |
   |---|---|---|
   | C | 1 × 2 = 2 | 2 × 1 = 2 |
   | H | 1 × 4 = 4 | 2 × 2 = 4 |
   | O | 3 × 2 = 6 | (2 × 2 from CO2) + (2 × 1 from H2O) = 6 |

3. One construction starts with coefficient 1 for C2H4. Its two C require coefficient 2 for CO2; its four H require coefficient 2 for H2O. Those products together contain six O, requiring coefficient 3 for O2. The final table rechecks all three elements after these choices. It certifies the stated element balance, not an actual product yield or reaction rate.

### Case 4 — Scale every coefficient together

1. P and Q both conserve Al and Cl:

   | Vector | Al: left = right | Cl: left = right | Status before correction |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | P: `(1, 3/2, 1)` | 1 = 1 | (3/2) × 2 = 1 × 3 = 3 | Balanced intermediate; one coefficient is not a whole number |
   | Q: `(6, 9, 6)` | 6 = 6 | 9 × 2 = 6 × 3 = 18 | Balanced positive integers; a common factor remains |

   P is not a permitted final vector here because of its fraction. Q satisfies the positivity and integer requirements, but still needs normalization under the stated reporting rule.

2. Multiplying **every** entry of P by 2 gives `(2, 3, 2)`. The gcd of Q is 3; dividing **every** entry of Q by 3 also gives `(2, 3, 2)`. Thus they represent the same coefficient ratio.

   **Normalized equation: `2 Al + 3 Cl2 → 2 AlCl3`.**

   | Element | Left total | Right total |
   |---|---|---|
   | Al | 2 × 1 = 2 | 2 × 1 = 2 |
   | Cl | 3 × 2 = 6 | 2 × 3 = 6 |

   The normalized vector has gcd 1.

3. Changing only the middle entry makes `(1, 3, 1)`. Its Al counts remain 1 and 1, but its Cl counts are **3 × 2 = 6 on the left and 1 × 3 = 3 on the right**. It is unbalanced. Scaling one entry alone changes the relative quantities; multiplying all entries by 2 would instead multiply every element total on both sides by the same factor.

### Case 5 — Expand the groups in the final check

1. A normalized vector is **`(1, 2, 1, 2)`**:

   **`Ca(OH)2 + 2 HNO3 → Ca(NO3)2 + 2 H2O`**

   One Ca on the left calls for one Ca(NO3)2 on the right. That product has two N, supplied by two HNO3. The resulting H count calls for two H2O. These choices still need the full O check below.

2. Expanding the formulas gives:

   | Element | Left total | Right total |
   |---|---|---|
   | Ca | 1 × 1 from Ca(OH)2 = 1 | 1 × 1 from Ca(NO3)2 = 1 |
   | N | 2 × 1 from HNO3 = 2 | 1 × (2 × 1) from Ca(NO3)2 = 2 |
   | H | (1 × 2 from Ca(OH)2) + (2 × 1 from HNO3) = 4 | 2 × 2 from H2O = 4 |
   | O | (1 × 2 from Ca(OH)2) + (2 × 3 from HNO3) = 8 | (1 × (2 × 3) from Ca(NO3)2) + (2 × 1 from H2O) = 8 |

   Each element agrees. The coefficient vector includes 1, so its gcd is 1.

3. Normalization acts on the four coefficients together, not on the formulas. The subscripts in Ca(OH)2 and Ca(NO3)2 specify how much of each element one formula unit represents. Changing those subscripts changes the stipulated composition instead of removing a common scale from the equation. The OH and NO3 groups must still be expanded into their constituent element counts when verifying conservation.

### Case 6 — A contradiction rules out every positive pair

1. For **`a SO2 → b SO3`**, the necessary equalities are:

   - S conservation: **a = b**.
   - O conservation: **2a = 3b**.

2. Substituting b = a into the O equation gives 2a = 3a, hence a = 0 and therefore b = 0. This contradicts the required strict positivity. Consequently **no allowed positive coefficient pair balances this fixed skeleton**. The argument covers every possible positive pair; it does not depend on having tried a few numbers unsuccessfully.

3. The vector `(0, 0)` gives zero counts on both sides but deletes both specified substances, contrary to the task. Adding another substance would change the skeleton and answer a different question. This impossibility concerns only the restricted two-formula equation. It does not say that SO3 cannot participate in other chemical reactions.

### Case 7 — Two normalized ratios can remain different

1. The element checks are:

   | Vector | Element | Left total | Right total |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | U: `(5, 3, 4, 1)` | H | 5 × 2 = 10 | (4 × 2 from H2O) + (1 × 2 from H2O2) = 10 |
   | U | O | 3 × 2 = 6 | (4 × 1 from H2O) + (1 × 2 from H2O2) = 6 |
   | V: `(5, 4, 2, 3)` | H | 5 × 2 = 10 | (2 × 2 from H2O) + (3 × 2 from H2O2) = 10 |
   | V | O | 4 × 2 = 8 | (2 × 1 from H2O) + (3 × 2 from H2O2) = 8 |

   Both vectors are positive integer balances of the specified skeleton. U has gcd 1 because one entry is 1. V also has gcd 1: already its entries 5 and 4 have no common divisor greater than 1.

2. If multiplying U by one positive factor produced V, their equal first entries would require **5 × factor = 5**, so the factor would be 1. But that would leave the second entry 3, not change it to 4. The vectors are therefore **not scalar multiples**.

3. The uniqueness claim is false for this skeleton. Removing a common factor eliminates redundant scaling of a particular ratio; it does not exclude a different ratio with different relative amounts of the two products. U and V are explicit normalized counterexamples to the claim. No enumeration of all possible ratios is needed.

4. The counts establish two admissible algebraic balances, not two experimentally established outcomes. They do not tell us whether a real mixture forms either product combination, which ratio it would produce, or under what conditions. Choosing a chemically observed outcome requires information beyond element conservation.

- [§4.1, Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations): coefficients versus fixed subscripts, per-element checks, fractional intermediates and common-factor reduction.

- [§2.6, Ionic and Molecular Compounds](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/2-6-ionic-and-molecular-compounds): molecular versus ionic composition and parenthesized groups.
